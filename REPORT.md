## 0. Scope (from the H1 plan)

**Problem (from H1):** About 32% of the 4,424 students in my dataset dropped out. At the end of semester 1, a first-year academic advisor who has time to contact roughly 100 students must decide whom to contact first. My system ranks students by estimated dropout risk using only information available at that point, so there is still time to help before semester 2. It does not explain why students leave or recommend interventions.

**Technique(s) (from H1):** Logistic regression implemented from scratch (sigmoid, log loss with L2 regularisation, full-batch gradient descent), with the decision threshold tuned on validation data to favour recall, and verified against scikit-learn. It is compared with a majority-class baseline and a semester-1 rule (flag students who passed fewer than half of their enrolled units).

**Data (from H1):** UCI "Predict Students' Dropout and Academic Success" (Realinho et al., 2021, CC BY 4.0): 4,424 anonymised students from the Polytechnic Institute of Portalegre (2008/09–2018/19), 36 features plus the target, no missing values. The label is converted to Dropout vs Not Dropout (Graduate + Enrolled), the six semester-2 columns are removed to prevent leakage, and the data are split 60/20/20 with stratification.

**What makes this HD-level (from H1):** A real user with a capacity constraint (measured with precision@100), a leakage-aware design, a from-scratch optimiser verified against a library, per-student explanations for the advisor, and a fairness audit (with vs without sensitive features) judged against six success criteria fixed in H1 before any results were seen.

**Pivot since H1 (if any):** No change to the problem, data or technique. ## 1. Problem Definition and Motivation

About a third of the students in my dataset (1,421 of 4,424) left their programme before finishing. Support exists, but an advisor cannot meet every first-year student, so the real question is one of prioritisation: at the end of semester 1, when first results are known but semester 2 has not started, which students should be contacted first? A warning that arrives later is useless, so the system may only use information available at that moment.

The obvious alternative to AI is a hand-written rule, and I built one: flag anyone who passed fewer than half of their semester-1 units. By construction, this rule cannot see students who pass their units and still leave, and in my test set they are 42% of all dropouts (119 of 284). Catching them means weighing many weak signals together (fees, age, course, grades, enrolment pattern), and those weights cannot sensibly be set by hand. Logistic regression learns them from past outcomes, outputs a probability that can be ranked, and keeps every weight readable, so the advisor can be shown why a student was flagged. Because the output affects real students, I fixed six pass/fail success criteria in H1, including a fairness criterion, so the project is able to fail honestly.

## 2. Technical Approach

**Data pipeline.** The six semester-2 columns are dropped before splitting, and an assertion stops the notebook if any remain. I added `sem1_pass_rate` (approved ÷ enrolled units) and a `sem1_no_units` flag: students enrolled in zero units would otherwise cause a division by zero, so they get a rate of 0 plus a flag the model can weight separately. Nine columns are numeric codes for categories (course, parents' qualification, etc.), so treating them as numbers would impose a meaningless order; I one-hot encode them and merge levels with fewer than 30 training students into "other" so no weight is learned from a handful of people. The `Preprocessor` class learns category levels, means and standard deviations from the training split only, and the stratified 60/20/20 split keeps the 32% dropout rate in every part.

**Model.** I chose logistic regression over Naive Bayes, whose independence assumption would double-count my strongly correlated semester-1 features (units enrolled, approved and grade), and over a neural network, which would be harder to explain and audit for bias with only 2,654 training rows. `LogisticRegressionGD` computes z = w·x + b, applies a numerically stable sigmoid (it never exponentiates a large positive number), and minimises clipped log loss plus an L2 penalty that excludes the bias. Full-batch gradient descent uses the analytic gradient Xᵀ(p − y)/n + λw. Training stops on convergence (loss change < 10⁻⁷) or divergence (non-finite loss, or 10× the starting loss), so a bad learning rate is reported instead of crashing. I chose full-batch over stochastic descent because 2,654 training rows is small and a deterministic loss curve is easier to interpret (Figure 1).

**Decisions.** The threshold is not 0.5: `tune_threshold` picks, on validation data only, the threshold with the best F1 among those reaching recall ≥ 0.70, because a missed dropout costs more than a false alarm. Hyperparameters are selected by validation log loss, and the test set is used once. To verify correctness I mapped my objective onto scikit-learn's (C = 1/(λn)) and compared predictions. For the advisor, `top_contributors` turns weight × standardised value into the three features that raised each student's risk. Comparisons: majority class, the semester-1 rule, and my model retrained without the seven sensitive columns (gender, nationality, international, age, debtor, tuition fees, scholarship).

## 3. Results and Analysis

**Experiment 1: optimisation.** Figure 1(a) shows the learning-rate trade-off, but in an unexpected place. Rates of 0.001 and 0.01 are still decreasing after 3,000 iterations, 0.1 converges in 1,203, and 1.0 reaches the same loss (0.3385) in only 187. A step of 1.0 sounded reckless, but standardisation keeps the curvature of the loss small; only at 5 and 20 does each step overshoot, so the loss oscillates (final 0.59 and 4.74). Figure 1(b) shows training loss rising steadily with λ while validation loss is lowest at λ = 0.01 (0.351); at λ = 1 validation F1 collapses to 0.51 (underfitting). λ = 0.001 had a slightly higher validation F1 (0.767 vs 0.759), but I kept the fixed selection rule (log loss) rather than switch after seeing results.

**Experiment 2: test results.** The model reaches recall 0.764, F1 0.786 and precision@100 0.99, against 0.581, 0.678 and 0.84 for the rule; the majority class scores 67.9% accuracy with zero recall, so accuracy alone is misleading. Figure 2(a) turns this into advisor effort: after 100 contacts the model has reached 99 of 284 dropouts, versus 84 for the rule and about 32 at random. The gain comes from "hidden" dropouts who passed at least half their units: the rule catches none of the 119, the model 51.3%, while flagging 6.2% of non-dropouts who also passed (Experiment 3b). The model agrees with scikit-learn on 100% of test predictions, and across five random splits F1 ranges from 0.727 to 0.790, beating the rule every time. Criteria 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 are met.

**What failed.** Criterion 4 is not. Figure 2(b) shows recall of 0.517 for scholarship holders, 24.7 points below overall, and 0.627 for students aged ≤ 20. I expected removing sensitive features to fix this, as H1 planned. It did not: the scholarship gap barely moved (−24.1 points), while F1 fell from 0.786 to 0.705. Investigating why, I found scholarship dropouts had much better semester-1 records (mean pass rate 0.51 vs 0.35) and paid fees on time more often, so they look like successful students on exactly the features that dominate the model; the gap comes from academic signals, not the sensitive column. Only 29 scholarship holders dropped out, but the 95% bootstrap interval [0.34, 0.69] stays below the overall 0.764. The error analysis shows the same pattern: among the 20 most confidently missed dropouts, 85% passed at least 80% of their units with fees paid. Following my H1 rule, I do not call the system successful: it works on average but is unsafe for scholarship holders and young students.

## 4. Limitations and Future Work

1. **Invisible dropouts.** Students who passed at least 80% of their units and paid their fees carry almost no risk signal, so some who later leave get a risk of 10% or less. This is a data limit, not a tuning problem: their reasons (financial change, mental health, switching course) are not recorded at the end of semester 1.
2. **One global threshold.** Scholarship holders have a lower base rate and a negative scholarship weight (−0.25), so a single threshold of 0.41 systematically under-flags them.
3. **Cohort proxies.** The largest positive weight is the national unemployment rate (0.29), identical for everyone who enrolled in the same year, so the model partly learns cohort membership, which will not transfer to future years.
4. **Missing-data proxies.** "Mother's qualification = 34" (the dataset's "unknown" code) appears among the reasons shown to the advisor, so an incomplete record is treated as risk.
5. **Linearity, label and setting.** Interactions such as age × evening attendance are not modelled; course switchers count as dropouts; and the data come from one Portuguese polytechnic (2008–2019).

**Future work:** subgroup-aware thresholds evaluated against equal opportunity, a time-based split (train on early cohorts, test on later ones) to measure drift, and a second check early in semester 2 for students who look safe at the end of semester 1.

## 5. Ethical Reflection

The people affected are first-year students and their advisors. The most harmful failure is a false negative: a missed student silently receives nothing, while a false positive costs one conversation. The C2 concept that frames my results is **equal opportunity**: a fair screening tool should have a similar true-positive rate (recall) across groups. My model violates it for scholarship holders and students aged ≤ 20. The common remedy, **fairness through unawareness** (deleting sensitive features), failed here because the gap flows through academic features that correlate with group membership.

This exposes a specific tension. Tuition-fee status is both the model's strongest single risk signal and a marker of financial hardship. Keeping it means 4 of the 10 most confident false alarms were flagged mainly for being behind on fees, which risks treating poverty as a warning sign. Removing it lowers precision from 0.81 to about 0.64, so advisors spend more of their limited time on students who were fine, and scholarship holders are still under-served. Neither option is neutral.

From C4, a **model card** for this system would need to state the intended use (prioritising supportive check-ins, never admission, scholarship or disciplinary decisions), report subgroup recall next to overall metrics, and warn against automation bias: a low score is not evidence that a student is safe. Advisors should review scholarship holders and young students regardless of their rank.
